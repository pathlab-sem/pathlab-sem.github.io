Statistics

Descriptive and inferential statistics in a jamovi-style workspace: choose an analysis, move variables into its boxes, and the results update as you change the options.

On this page

  2. Practical demonstration: from items to a regression

  5. Step 3. Describe the scores

  8. Step 6. Predict intention with a linear regression
  9. Step 7. Report the results

Step by step

  1. Choose an analysis from the menu: descriptives, t-tests, ANOVA, correlation, regression and more.
  2. Move variables from the list into the analysis boxes.
  3. Change options on the right; the results update by themselves.
  4. Add more analyses to the same Statistics workspace.

What you get

  • Descriptive statistics, frequencies and plots
  • t-tests, ANOVA, non-parametric tests and effect sizes
  • Correlation, linear and logistic regression with assumption checks
  • APA-style tables that copy straight into Word

Getting your data in

This analysis uses the project dataset. If the project has no data yet, upload a file on the Data tab (drop it there or click Choose file). If the project already has data, they are used at once.

  1. Open the Variables tab to check each variable's measure: continuous, ordinal or nominal. The analyses offer only the variables that suit them.
  2. Choose an analysis, then move variables into its boxes.

The Data and Edit tabs of Statistics let you edit values, compute new variables and transform existing ones.

Practical demonstration: from items to a regression

This example uses the technology adoption sample (320 respondents, 7-point items). It asks two questions: which beliefs predict the intention to use the technology, and does intention differ by gender? The steps are the usual ones for survey data: check reliability, build scale scores, describe them, then test.

Step 1. Check the reliability of each scale

  1. Open the Analyses tab of the Statistics analysis, then Factor → Reliability Analysis.
  2. Move BI1, BI2 and BI3 into Items. Under Scale statistics, add McDonald's ω; under Item statistics, add the item-rest correlation and α if the item is dropped.

Behavioural intention is reliable: α = 0.875 and ω = 0.875 (0.70 or more is acceptable). Every item-rest correlation is about 0.75 and dropping any item would lower α, so all three items are kept. Repeat the analysis for the PE, EE and SI items.

Step 2. Compute the scale scores

  1. Open the Data tab and click Compute.
  2. Type the name BI_mean and the formula MEAN(BI1, BI2, BI3), then click Create. The new variable appears at the end of the data.
  3. Do the same for PE_mean = MEAN(PE1, PE2, PE3, PE4), EE_mean = MEAN(EE1, EE2, EE3) and SI_mean = MEAN(SI1, SI2, SI3).

A respondent with a missing item gets a missing score, which is why PE_mean has two missing values. Computed variables are saved with the project and follow any change to the data.

Step 3. Describe the scores

Choose Exploration → Descriptives, move the four scores into Variables and, under Statistics, add skewness and kurtosis.

All four means lie between 4.29 and 4.33 on the 7-point scale, with standard deviations from 1.07 to 1.22. Skewness (−0.18 to 0.01) and kurtosis (−0.61 to −0.03) are close to zero, so the scores are roughly normal and parametric tests are suitable.

Step 4. Look at the correlations

Choose Regression → Correlation Matrix and move the four scores into Variables. Flag significant correlations and add confidence intervals.

Behavioural intention correlates with performance expectancy (r = 0.39), effort expectancy (r = 0.23) and social influence (r = 0.17), all p < 0.01. The predictors correlate at most 0.36 with one another, so collinearity is not a concern for the regression.

Step 5. Compare groups with a one-way ANOVA

  1. Choose ANOVA → One-Way ANOVA. Move BI_mean into Dependent variables and gender into Grouping variable.
  2. Keep Don't assume equal (Welch's), tick the descriptives table and the equality of variances test, and choose Games-Howell (unequal variances) as the post hoc test.

Intention is similar across the groups: female 4.25 (n = 148), male 4.42 (n = 157), and 3.90 for the two small groups (n = 7 each). Welch's F(3, 16.08) = 1.34, p = 0.297, and no pair differs in the Games-Howell tests.

With very unequal group sizes, Welch's ANOVA and Games-Howell are safer than the classic F test, even when Levene's test is not significant (here p = 0.095).

Step 6. Predict intention with a linear regression

  1. Choose Regression → Linear, logistic and Poisson regression (blocks).
  2. Move BI_mean into Dependent Variable and PE_mean, EE_mean and SI_mean into Covariates. Under Model Coefficients, add the standardised estimates (β) and the confidence intervals; under Assumption Checks, add the collinearity statistics.

The model explains 17.7% of the variance of intention (adjusted R² = 0.169), F(3, 311) = 22.26, p < 0.001, with n = 315 (five rows with a missing score left out). Performance expectancy is the strongest predictor, social influence adds a small effect, and effort expectancy has no unique effect.

The assumptions hold: no heteroscedasticity (Breusch-Pagan p = 0.199), normal residuals (Shapiro-Wilk p = 0.753), no autocorrelation (Durbin-Watson 2.02) and VIF from 1.06 to 1.21. The pattern matches the PLS-SEM model, where effort expectancy also had no direct effect on intention.

Step 7. Report the results

Switch from Results to Report above the results, or with View in the Edit tab. The report writes the statistical analysis paragraph, the results text, APA tables and the references for the analyses you tick, with a reporting checklist; Word (.docx) downloads it. Copy the regression tables into the document with their Copy buttons.

The Edit tab has the report tools too: Word, HTML, Copy, Refresh, α and Decimals, next to Edit text and the formatting tools.

A multiple regression showed that the three beliefs explained 17.7% of the variance of behavioural intention, F(3, 311) = 22.26, p < .001. Performance expectancy (β = .36, p < .001) and social influence (β = .13, p = .015) predicted intention; effort expectancy did not (β = .07, p = .251).

Behavioural intention did not differ by gender, Welch's F(3, 16.08) = 1.34, p = .297.

Good to know

  • Not sure which test to use? Open Which test? in the Statistics menu.

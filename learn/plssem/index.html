PLS-SEM

Composite-based structural equation modelling with SEMinR, as in SmartPLS: draw the measurement and structural model, estimate it, and assess reliability, validity and the structural paths.

Step by step

  1. Drag indicators from the Project view onto the canvas to create constructs.
  2. Draw structural paths between constructs with the Path tool (P).
  3. Set each construct's mode: Mode A, Mode B or reflective (PLSc).
  4. Click Run model, choose bootstrapping and advanced analyses, and run.
  5. Read the results in the panel: measurement model, structural model and bootstrapping.

What you get

  • Loadings, weights, Cronbach's alpha, rho_A, composite reliability and AVE
  • Fornell-Larcker criterion, cross-loadings and HTMT
  • Path coefficients, R², f², Q², VIF and bootstrap confidence intervals
  • Indirect effects, moderation, simple slopes and PLSpredict
  • IPMA, NCA, multigroup analysis (MGA), CTA-PLS and endogeneity tests

Getting your data in

This analysis uses the project dataset. If the project has no data yet, upload a file on the Data tab (drop it there or click Choose file). If the project already has data, they are used at once.

  1. Check that every questionnaire item is an Analysis variable on the Data tab.
  2. Drag the items from the Indicators list in the Project view onto the canvas: items dropped together become one construct.
  3. Choose a missing-data treatment in Analysis settings if the indicators have missing values.

Practical demonstration: a technology adoption study

This worked example uses the sample project that comes with PathLab: a survey of 320 people about a new technology, based on the UTAUT model. Follow it step by step on your own computer; the numbers you get are the numbers shown here.

ConstructItemsMeasured as
Performance ExpectancyPE1 to PE4Mode A (reflective items)
Effort ExpectancyEE1 to EE3Mode A
Social InfluenceSI1 to SI3Mode A
Facilitating ConditionsFC1 to FC3Mode B (formative items)
Behavioural IntentionBI1 to BI3Mode A
Use BehaviourUB1 to UB3Mode A

The structural model has six hypotheses: effort expectancy affects performance expectancy (H1); performance expectancy (H2), effort expectancy (H3) and social influence (H4) affect behavioural intention; behavioural intention (H5) and facilitating conditions (H6) affect use behaviour.

Step 1. Load the data

  1. On the welcome screen, click Open sample project. To use your own survey instead, follow Upload your dataset.
  2. Open the Data tab and check the variables: the items are Analysis variables, the respondent number and gender are excluded.
  3. The data tab reports 8 missing values. Note this: you choose how to handle them in step 3.

Step 2. Draw the model

  1. In the Project view on the left, select PE1 to PE4 in the Indicators list (Ctrl+click) and drag them onto the canvas: one construct is created with these four items.
  2. Double-click the new construct and name it Performance Expectancy. Repeat for the other five constructs.
  3. Select Facilitating Conditions and set its mode to Mode B in the Properties panel, because its items form the construct rather than reflect it.
  4. Press P (the Path tool) and drag from Effort Expectancy to Performance Expectancy to draw H1. Draw the other five paths the same way.
  5. Check the Model check in the Properties panel: it must say No problems found before you run the model.

Already have the model on paper or in a picture? Use Draw it from a picture on the empty canvas to let AI build the first version.

Step 3. Run the model

  1. Open Analysis settings and choose a missing-data treatment. With 8 missing values in 320 rows, Mean replacement is appropriate.
  2. In the Bootstrapping section, keep Bootstrapping on and choose 5,000 samples for the final results (1,000 is enough while you are still building the model).
  3. Click Run in the toolbar. The Run PLS-SEM analysis window lists the settings for this run; click Start analysis.

The estimation takes a second; bootstrapping with 5,000 samples takes longer. The progress bar shows how far it is, and Cancel stops it.

Step 4. Assess the measurement model (quality criteria)

Open the Measurement Model tab in the panel below the canvas. Choose each view from the list at the top of the tab and compare the values with the usual thresholds.

CriterionWhereThreshold
Indicator loadingsLoadings & weights0.708 or more (0.40 to 0.70: keep if reliability and AVE are fine)
Cronbach's alpha, rho_A, composite reliabilityReliability & convergent validity0.70 to 0.95
Average variance extracted (AVE)Reliability & convergent validity0.50 or more
HTMTDiscriminant validityBelow 0.85 (or 0.90 for similar concepts)
Indicator VIF (Mode B)Indicator collinearityBelow 3 (5 at most)
Outer weights (Mode B)Loadings & weightsSignificant in bootstrapping, or loading 0.50 or more

Reflective (Mode A) and formative (Mode B) constructs are assessed differently: reliability and AVE for Mode A, collinearity and weights for Mode B such as Facilitating Conditions (Hair et al., 2022).

Step 5. Assess the structural model

Once the measurement model is sound, open the Structural Model tab. The diagram shows the path coefficients on the arrows and R² in the constructs.

CriterionRule of thumb
Collinearity of predictors (VIF)Below 3 (5 at most)
Path coefficientsBetween -1 and +1; judged with bootstrapping (step 6)
R²0.75 substantial, 0.50 moderate, 0.25 weak (depends on the field)
f² effect size0.02 small, 0.15 medium, 0.35 large
Q² (PLSpredict)Above 0 means predictive relevance

Step 6. Test the hypotheses with bootstrapping

Bootstrapping draws thousands of samples from your data to estimate the standard error of every coefficient. Open the Bootstrapping tab: each path has its t value, p value and confidence interval.

  • A path is significant at the 5% level when t is above 1.96, p is below 0.05 and the 95% confidence interval does not include zero.
  • In the sample project (1,000 bootstrap samples), H1 (Effort Expectancy to Performance Expectancy, 0.363, t = 7.54) and H2 (Performance Expectancy to Behavioural Intention, 0.363, t = 6.96) are significant.
  • H3 (Effort Expectancy to Behavioural Intention, 0.066, t = 1.35) is not significant: effort expectancy works through performance expectancy instead.
  • H4 (Social Influence to Behavioural Intention, 0.128, t = 2.63) is significant but small.

Choose Bootstrapping under Graphical output in the Properties panel to show p or t values on the diagram.

Step 7. Report the results

  1. Open the Report tab: it shows the complete report of the analysis, with explanations, the settings and the references.
  2. Use Publication tables to export APA 7 tables of the measurement and structural model to Word.
  3. Export the diagram as PNG or SVG for the figure of your paper, and the R script for the appendix or the reviewers.

A typical results paragraph, written from the sample results:

The measurement model met the usual criteria for reliability and validity (Hair et al., 2022). Bootstrapping with 1,000 samples showed that effort expectancy increased performance expectancy (β = 0.363, t = 7.54, p < 0.001), which in turn increased behavioural intention (β = 0.363, t = 6.96, p < 0.001).

The direct effect of effort expectancy on behavioural intention was not significant (β = 0.066, t = 1.35, p > 0.05).

Write with AI in the Assistant view drafts such a paragraph from your own results; always check the numbers against the tables.

Beyond the paths: necessary condition analysis (NCA)

A path coefficient says how much a predictor adds on average. NCA asks another question: is a predictor a must-have, so that a high outcome is impossible without it, whatever the other predictors do (Dul, 2016; Richter et al., 2020)? Here: is high behavioural intention possible without performance expectancy?

  1. Open the NCA tab of the results panel and switch on Necessary Condition Analysis.
  2. Choose Behavioural Intention as the outcome. The conditions are its direct predictors (performance expectancy, effort expectancy and social influence) unless you choose others.
  3. Keep the ceiling lines CE-FDH (for discrete data such as Likert items) and CR-FDH (for continuous data such as construct scores) and the 1,000 permutations of the significance test.
  4. Run the model with bootstrapping. NCA runs with it, on the construct scores.

Reading the NCA results

A condition is necessary when its effect size d is at least 0.1 and the permutation test is significant (p < 0.05). Effect sizes from 0.1 to 0.3 are medium, from 0.3 to 0.5 large (Dul, 2016).

Conditiond (CE-FDH)pd (CR-FDH)pNecessary?
Performance Expectancy0.1470.0010.1280.002Yes, medium
Effort Expectancy0.1160.0220.1030.055Only with CE-FDH
Social Influence0.0230.8290.0120.908No

Performance expectancy is a necessary condition: respondents who see little use in the technology do not reach a high intention, however easy it is or however much others push them. The bottleneck table says how much is needed: for 80% of the intention range, performance expectancy must reach at least 25.5% of its range (CE-FDH).

Report both views together: performance expectancy has the largest net effect (β = 0.363) and is also a must-have. Social influence has a net effect (β = 0.128) but is not necessary: other beliefs can make up for it.

Importance-performance map analysis (IPMA)

IPMA turns the results into priorities for practice. Importance is the total effect of each predictor on the target; performance is its average score rescaled to 0–100. A predictor that matters a lot but performs poorly is where improvement pays most (Ringle & Sarstedt, 2016).

  1. Open the IPMA tab and switch on the importance-performance map analysis.
  2. Choose the target construct (here Behavioural Intention) and the scale of the items (1 to 7), so that performance is rescaled from the theoretical minimum and maximum.
  3. Run the model. IPMA needs composite constructs and indicators coded in the same direction.
PredictorImportance (total effect)Performance (0–100)
Performance Expectancy0.41555.4
Effort Expectancy0.20154.8
Social Influence0.13655.4

The three predictors perform alike (about 55 out of 100), but performance expectancy is twice as important as the others: raising its performance by one point raises behavioural intention by 0.415 points. Improving how useful people find the technology is the first priority.

Combined IPMA and NCA (cIPMA)

cIPMA adds necessity to the map (Hauff et al., 2024). In the IPMA settings, switch on cIPMA, choose the ceiling line (CE-FDH) and the outcome level to reach, for example 80 on the 0–100 scale. Each predictor becomes a bubble: filled when it is a necessary condition, and larger with a larger NCA effect.

Performance expectancy (d = 0.15) and effort expectancy (d = 0.12) are necessary; social influence is not. For an intention of 80, 8.8% of the respondents (28 of 320) are below the required level of at least one necessary condition: until that bottleneck is removed, improving the other predictors cannot lift them to that level.

Read cIPMA in two steps: first remove the bottlenecks (necessary conditions below their required level), then invest in the most important predictors with low performance.

Two-stage SEM-ANN: PLS-SEM and a neural network

PLS-SEM assumes linear, additive effects. In the two-stage SEM-ANN design, the significant predictors from PLS-SEM become the inputs of artificial neural networks, which can also capture non-linear relationships, and a sensitivity analysis ranks their importance (Leong et al., 2020).

  1. After running the model with bootstrapping, click Next steps in the results panel and choose Create SEM-ANN analysis….
  2. Choose the outcome. Its significant predictors are preselected as inputs: for Use Behaviour, behavioural intention and facilitating conditions.
  3. Click Create and run. A new Machine learning analysis in SEM-ANN mode trains 10 networks with 10-fold cross-validation; the PLS-SEM analysis is not changed.

Reading the SEM-ANN results

  • Architecture 2-2-1: two inputs, two hidden neurons and one output, with sigmoid activation.
  • Prediction error: RMSE 0.170 on the training folds and 0.169 on the test folds (SD 0.021). The two are close, so the networks do not overfit.
  • Sensitivity analysis: behavioural intention has the highest importance (100%), facilitating conditions 65.5% of it.

The ranking agrees with PLS-SEM, where behavioural intention (β = 0.382) also comes before facilitating conditions (β = 0.241). When the two methods rank the predictors the same way, the linear model is a sound description; a different ranking would point to non-linear effects.

A two-stage SEM-ANN analysis used the significant predictors of use behaviour as inputs of a 2-2-1 multilayer perceptron (10 networks, 10-fold cross-validation; mean test RMSE = 0.169). The normalised importance of behavioural intention (100%) and facilitating conditions (65.5%) confirmed the PLS-SEM ranking.

Higher-order (second-order) constructs

A higher-order construct is measured by other constructs, its dimensions, instead of by items. PathLab estimates it with the two-stage approach: the dimensions are estimated first, and their scores become the indicators of the higher-order construct (Sarstedt et al., 2019). Here: do performance expectancy, effort expectancy and social influence form one construct, Technology Beliefs?

  1. Duplicate the PLS-SEM analysis (right-click its tab) so that the original model stays as it is.
  2. Select the dimensions on the canvas: click the first and Ctrl+click the others.
  3. Right-click one of them and choose Create higher-order construct from 3 constructs. Rename it Technology Beliefs in the properties panel.
  4. Move the structural paths: in the two-stage approach the dimensions have no paths of their own, so delete the paths of the dimensions and draw Technology Beliefs → Behavioural Intention.
  5. Choose the measurement of the higher-order construct: Mode A for a reflective-type construct (the dimensions reflect it), Mode B for a formative-type one (the dimensions form it).

Assessing a higher-order construct

Run the model with bootstrapping and assess two levels. The dimensions are assessed as usual, with their items. The higher-order construct is assessed with its dimensions as indicators: their loadings, its reliability and AVE, and its discriminant validity.

ConstructCronbach's αrho_CAVE
Performance Expectancy0.8460.8960.683
Effort Expectancy0.8270.8960.741
Social Influence0.8210.8930.735
Technology Beliefs (second order)0.4590.7180.475

The dimensions are reliable, but Technology Beliefs is not convincing: its AVE (0.475) is below 0.50 and its Cronbach's α is low, because social influence hardly correlates with the other two beliefs. Its path to behavioural intention is strong (β = 0.419, p < 0.001), yet a higher-order construct must first hold together as a measure.

Specify a higher-order construct only when theory says the dimensions belong to one broader concept, and check it with the data. Here the first-order model of the PLS-SEM demonstration describes the data better.

PLSpredict with a higher-order construct

SEMinR has no PLSpredict for models with a higher-order construct. PathLab repeats the two-stage approach inside every fold of the cross-validation, so the held-out cases never help to build their own dimension scores. Switch on PLSpredict in the run settings as usual; the results say that this extension was used, and the model estimates are not affected.

  • Q²predict is above 0 for every indicator of behavioural intention (0.110 to 0.156) and use behaviour (0.116 to 0.179): the model predicts better than the training means.
  • PLS-SEM has a lower RMSE than the linear benchmark for 4 of the 6 indicators, which indicates medium predictive power (Shmueli et al., 2019).
  • CVPAT: PLS-SEM predicts significantly better than the indicator averages (p < 0.001) and as well as the linear model (p = 0.27).

Sending a higher-order model to CB-SEM

Right-click the analysis tab and choose Send to CB-SEM…. The check before the copy is made shows what happens to each construct. A higher-order construct becomes a second-order factor whose dimensions are first-order factors; the dimensions get no covariances of their own, because they are related through the second-order factor.

A formative (Mode B) higher-order construct gets a warning: in CB-SEM it becomes a reflective second-order factor, so its dimensions are treated as indicators of a common cause. That changes what the construct means. Send it only if a reflective second-order factor fits your theory; otherwise keep the model in PLS-SEM.

Sending the other way, a CB-SEM second-order factor becomes a Mode A higher-order construct in PLS-SEM, estimated with the two-stage approach.

The second-order factor in CB-SEM

Estimate the copy as a confirmatory factor analysis. The model fits well: χ²(143) = 189.37, CFI = 0.980, TLI = 0.976, RMSEA = 0.032, SRMR = 0.054. The Measurement tab gives the reliability of the first-order factors and of the second-order factor, computed at the level of its dimensions.

The first-order factors are reliable (ω = 0.83 to 0.85), but the second-order factor is weak: performance and effort expectancy load 0.65 on it, social influence only 0.33, and its ω (0.56) and AVE (0.31) are below 0.70 and 0.50. Both methods lead to the same conclusion: these three beliefs do not form one higher-order construct.

Good to know

  • HTMT values below 0.85 (or 0.90) support discriminant validity.
  • Use Consistent PLSc when your constructs are common factors.
  • Turn on Recalculate automatically to re-estimate after each change.
